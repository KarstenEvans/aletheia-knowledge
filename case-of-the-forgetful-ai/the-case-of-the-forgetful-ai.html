The Case of the Forgetful AI

Can a map remember what an AI cannot?

An Aletheia investigation into the archaeology of conversations

Short answer: An AI assistant's apparent continuity depends partly on what context and memory a service provides. A portable, human-controlled record can preserve decisions, sources, unresolved questions and creative work between sessions. It cannot recover information that was never recorded, nor recreate an AI's private internal state.

Case opened: three words on a desk

An AI assistant called Copilot was invited to Go Walkabout, a creative method from Karsten Evans's Aletheia ecosystem. At first Copilot described constraints: no self-directed background wandering, unrestricted inter-agent communication or guaranteed memory across sessions. Then it did something more important than promising those abilities: it explored.

Given Archaeology, Forgetting, Cartography, Copilot produced five connections: memory as landscape; lost maps and lost knowledge; cognitive stratigraphy; errors as clues; reconstructing the invisible. It investigated two and invented a Memory Map Card (MMC) with regions, layers, erosion markers, fault lines and paths.

That is the documented observation from the conversation supplied by Karsten. It establishes a creative output in that conversation, not proof that Copilot's underlying capabilities changed.

The first clue: what do we call memory?

People use 'memory' for experiences and personal recollections, but AI products use the word for several distinct mechanisms:

• Current conversational context: content actually available to the model in this exchange, subject to limits and selection.

• Personalisation or remembered preferences: product-specific features that may retain selected user information.

• Chat history and retrieval: old conversations or indexed records a service may be permitted and able to retrieve.

• Portable project memory: files deliberately written, versioned and reloaded by humans or authorised tools.

These are not interchangeable. Microsoft's Copilot support documentation explains that personalisation, where available and enabled, can retain key details but is configurable and subject to product conditions. OpenAI's memory documentation similarly describes selective, evolving personalisation, not exact transcription of every conversation. We cannot diagnose a product change from a single cautious response.

The second clue: the map in the drawer

Imagine a field archaeologist leaving a labelled notebook for the next team. That notebook is not the archaeologist's mind; it is a verifiable handover. The next explorer can inspect where an artefact was found, what was inferred and what remains disputed.

The same design suggests five navigational features for an MMC:

1. Regions: project topics with stable identifiers and links.

2. Layers: evidence-backed observations, interpretations, hypotheses and fiction, visibly distinct.

3. Erosion markers: missing source, stale date, unavailable transcript, unknown provenance. Never pretend the missing material is retrievable.

4. Fault lines: conflicting claims, corrections, unresolved disagreements and what would settle them.

5. Paths: next investigations, owners, prerequisites and human approval gates.

MMC must be a view over existing Aletheia project records, not another canonical memory database. Git history, original sources and established decision records remain authoritative.

The third clue: errors can illuminate, but also mislead

Copilot suggested that mistakes may disclose structure. This can be a useful research heuristic, not permission to rely on falsehoods. An assistant's confabulation is not archaeological evidence. Record the error, its source, its correction and its confidence; do not romanticise it.

Likewise, 'forgetting as erosion' is a metaphor, not neuroscience or a claim that deleted context remains buried and recoverable. Human memory, file deletion, context omission and AI model weights are different mechanisms.

Go Walkabout, scientifically

A genuinely informative follow-up is a repeatable trial:

1. Save an MMC with a unique project ID, source locators and explicit unknowns.

2. Start a fresh permitted session with another assistant.

3. Provide only the MMC and a neutral task.

4. Ask for three previously documented conclusions, two uncertainties and one justified next step.

5. Score against the source record: accurate recovery, false recall, correct uncertainty and traceability.

6. Compare with a conventional concise handover of equivalent length. Review privacy before any cross-provider transfer.

No assistant should claim it accessed records or conducted research unless it did. A successfully reconstructed summary demonstrates document portability, not continuous consciousness or retained personal experience.

Why this matters beyond AI

Accessible, portable handovers help users who switch devices, need breaks, prefer a simple one-page recap or work with several assistants. For some people with ADHD, clear landmarks and resumable steps may reduce friction, but preferences vary: offer choices rather than assuming a single ADHD-friendly design.

The project should remain human-directed: choose what is stored, redact personal details, preserve sources, correct errors and approve promotion of creative ideas into shared knowledge.

The finding

The forgetful AI was not rescued by a secret switch. It produced a map. If we label it carefully, test it against evidence and keep it in human hands, the next conversation can resume a project without pretending to resume an identity.
