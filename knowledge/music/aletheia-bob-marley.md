## Why Marley matters to the catchphrase project

Bob Marley repeatedly puts large ideas into **short, singable, ordinary language**: love, resistance, consolation, dignity, freedom, faith and historical memory. His catchphrases often work because they sound like things a group of people can say together.

The project must also keep context. Marley is not simply "feel-good reggae": spiritual, anti-colonial, Pan-African, historical and political meanings are central to many songs.

# Detailed phrase cards

## "One love"

**Song:** One Love / People Get Ready  
**Class:** CORE CATCHPHRASE on the 1984 UK single  
**Vibe:** unity · community · reconciliation · spirituality

Two words behave like both greeting and philosophy. "One" simplifies plurality; "love" supplies the value. The phrase travels easily because it is short enough for speech, print, chant and symbol.

**Chart:** UK No. 5 in 1984.

[Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/bob-marley-the-wailers-one-lovepeople-get-ready/)

### Context note
The later song title explicitly incorporates *People Get Ready*, connecting Marley's work with Curtis Mayfield. Preserve that songwriting/history connection rather than presenting the phrase in isolation.

---

## "Get up, stand up"

**Writers:** Bob Marley / Peter Tosh  
**Vibe:** resistance · dignity · action

Two physical movements become political language. The verbs matter: the song does not merely ask the listener to agree, but to change posture.

**Walkabout:** embodied metaphor; civil rights language; protest music; collective singing.

---

## "No woman, no cry"

**Class:** CORE CATCHPHRASE via the 1981 UK reissue  
**Vibe:** consolation · memory · endurance · community

The phrase is often misread by listeners unfamiliar with Jamaican speech. Its durability demonstrates that ambiguous syntax can make a hook travel, although Aletheia should explain the intended consoling sense rather than celebrate the misunderstanding.

**Chart:** 1981 UK release reached No. 8.

[Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/bob-marley-the-wailers-no-woman-no-cry-1981/)

### Credit caution
The song is formally credited to Vincent Ford. Accounts of authorship and royalty arrangements require careful sourcing; do not casually rewrite the publishing credit.

---

## "Could you be loved?"

**Class:** CORE CATCHPHRASE  
**Vibe:** worth · vulnerability · challenge · acceptance

Questions recruit the listener. "Could" introduces possibility and doubt; "you" makes the question personal.

**Chart:** UK No. 5.

[Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/bob-marley-the-wailers-could-you-be-loved/)

---

## "Three little birds"

**Class:** ARTIST PHRASE / enduring cultural phrase  
**Vibe:** reassurance · nature · smallness · safety

The image is tiny and concrete. That matters: a general reassurance becomes easier to remember when attached to three visible little creatures.

**Original UK peak:** No. 17.

[Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/bob-marley-the-wailers-three-little-birds/)

---

## "Buffalo Soldier"

**Class:** CORE CATCHPHRASE  
**Vibe:** history · displacement · survival · identity

The phrase points outside pop music into the history of Black soldiers in the U.S. Army after the Civil War. This is an ideal Aletheia walkabout: catchy title → historical term → contested national history → diaspora and identity.

**Chart:** UK No. 4.

[Official Charts](https://www.officialcharts.com/songs/bob-marley-the-wailers-buffalo-soldier/)

---

## "Redemption song"

**Vibe:** liberation · memory · responsibility · mental freedom

"Redemption" carries religious, political and personal meanings. Pairing it with "song" suggests that music itself can participate in liberation rather than merely describe it.

### Important walkabout
The song's mental-emancipation language echoes a famous idea associated with Marcus Garvey. Later research should trace the exact Garvey source, wording, date and the relationship carefully rather than treating resemblance as magic coincidence.

---

## "I shot the sheriff"

**Vibe:** confession · authority · ambiguity · story

The first-person construction starts halfway into a drama. Who is speaking? Why? What happened before? A good narrative catchphrase creates questions faster than it answers them.

# Knowledge lanes

## 1. Action verbs
Get up. Stand up. Wake up. Live. Unite. Marley frequently turns ideas into bodily or collective actions.

## 2. Reassurance without denial
*Three Little Birds* and *No Woman, No Cry* comfort without requiring a complicated vocabulary.

## 3. Love as social as well as romantic
*One Love* enlarges "love" into unity; *Could You Be Loved* turns it into a question of worth and judgement.

## 4. Freedom and mental liberation
*Redemption Song* links historical oppression with the mind. This branch should connect to Marcus Garvey only with properly sourced historical context.

## 5. History hidden inside hooks
*Buffalo Soldier* can lead a listener into Reconstruction-era U.S. military history, race, westward expansion and displacement.

## 6. Biblical language and Rastafari
*Exodus*, Jah references and other scriptural language need religious/historical explanation. Do not reduce Rastafari to decorative reggae vocabulary.

## 7. Pan-African thought
*Africa Unite* belongs in a broader history of Pan-Africanism and postcolonial politics.

## 8. Community singing
Many Marley hooks use repetition and simple vocabulary that work particularly well in crowds. That social singability is part of the memory mechanism.

# Research standard

Marley's songs are often quoted without Jamaican, Rastafari, colonial or historical context. Aletheia should do the opposite:
- preserve the memorable phrase,
- explain language carefully,
- identify formal songwriter credit,
- follow historical/religious references,
- distinguish documented intent from later interpretation,
- avoid presenting a universal "peace and love" caricature when the song is about oppression, rights or history.
